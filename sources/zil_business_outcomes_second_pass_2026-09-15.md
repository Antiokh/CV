# KC ZIL — business outcomes, second-pass debrief

**Date captured:** 2026-09-15
**Source type:** first-person debrief from Anton Nazarov
**Purpose:** preserve measurable operating and business outcomes surfaced during a second-pass review of the ZIL role.

## Evidence status

This file contains Anton's direct recollection. Exact numbers should be treated as first-person evidence until corroborated by reports, GLPI exports, procurement records, HR/IT records or other stored documents.

Personal names, conflict narratives and allegations are intentionally excluded. Only career-relevant facts are retained.

## Service transformation

Anton recalls that before the support redesign, requests could remain unresolved for very long periods and some employees used personal equipment because assigned workstations remained unusable.

After HelpDesk, remote support, request classification and standardization:

- both reaction time and resolution time were defined;
- routine issues were often resolved in roughly 10–15 minutes;
- relevant SLA classes used targets around 30 minutes;
- work was prioritized by urgency and business impact;
- remote support removed much of the delay caused by physical travel between offices.

Monthly request volume is recalled as initially roughly 300–600, later falling to roughly 150 over about 6–12 months as backlog was cleared, workstations were standardized, recurring causes were removed and users received digital-literacy / security training.

## Business continuity through standardized endpoints

The team moved users away from storing working data only on individual PCs.

The model used centralized identity, shared / backed-up storage, standardized workstations and remote administration. When an endpoint failed, another workstation could be supplied and the employee could continue using the same account and centrally stored data.

This reduced endpoint-specific dependency, data-loss risk and recovery time.

## Cross-department event workflow

A structured event-request process replaced fragmented phone, email and duplicated Excel coordination.

The workflow covered at least four recurring delivery functions: IT, lighting, sound and installation / technical setup.

It introduced:

- one event request;
- a shared calendar / event grid;
- equipment reservation;
- assigned ownership;
- status / history tracking;
- cross-department visibility.

Anton estimates roughly 300 events passed through the workflow during practical use.

## CRM and platform-fit decisions

Bitrix24 was implemented / piloted but proved too complex for the actual user base and institutional context. The organization later moved more process work toward Pyrus, which had a lower adoption barrier and fit workflow-style use cases better.

The business signal is not CRM installation itself, but willingness to reject a technically capable platform when adoption failed and redirect toward a simpler operating model.

## Identity lifecycle and offboarding

Anton recalls finding more than 160 mailboxes / accounts associated with former employees that remained active after departure.

He rewrote onboarding / offboarding procedures across IT and adjacent functions. The team introduced stale-account monitoring and HR-to-IT synchronization so access removal became part of the employee lifecycle instead of an informal cleanup task.

## License and certificate lifecycle

Before centralized tracking, some licenses / certificates were discovered to be expired only after business work was disrupted.

Anton recalls a case where an expired certificate affected financial / accounting reporting.

After licenses, keys and certificates were registered in GLPI with advance reminders, renewal work could start months before expiration, moving the process from emergency recovery to a planned renewal / procurement cycle.

## Cross-department workflow digitization

Anton worked directly with departments to identify processes that could be moved into structured digital workflows.

Examples recalled:

- accounting / finance — financing requests;
- legal — procurement requests and contract initiation / approval;
- procurement — coordination and preparation of procurement documentation;
- technical operations — service workflows through GLPI;
- adjacent HR / asset / finance interactions where IT was part of the process.

This supports business-process redesign and cross-functional workflow automation as a core management capability.

## Fractional / shared capability model

Anton states that nominal IT payroll did not simply fall; instead, specialist capability increased through fractional and shared staffing.

The clearest example was a remote infrastructure / DevOps specialist retained at a fraction of the cost Anton estimates for a comparable full-time market role. Installation capability was also shared with adjacent organizational functions rather than duplicated inside IT.

The safe executive signal is capability-based organizational design: obtaining deeper specialist coverage under tight payroll constraints without building a fully staffed conventional department.

## Procurement economics

Anton recalls responsibility for the full IT procurement lifecycle: supplier / solution research, requirements, 223-FZ process participation, acceptance, implementation through the team and final accountability for the result.

A concrete example involved choosing not to split out one overpriced line item because the additional procurement procedure would have created more organizational cost and delay than the price difference itself.

This supports total-cost-of-procurement reasoning rather than lowest-unit-price optimization.

## Physical-security outcome

Anton recalls approximately three theft investigations in which the rebuilt CCTV / retention capability was used to provide evidence to law enforcement, with property recovered in some cases.

The business signal is incident-response and loss-control capability, not camera installation itself.

## Key-person continuity and privileged-access governance

Anton clarifies that an earlier resume phrase about "password recovery" understated the problem.

The inherited environment depended too heavily on one administrator and insufficiently documented / shared privileged access. After a continuity failure, the organization had to recover access and rebuild parts of its operating knowledge.

The response included:

- restoring privileged access where possible;
- rebuilding documentation;
- centralizing credentials / ownership;
- removing single-person control over critical systems;
- redesigning identity, domain and infrastructure governance.

CV-safe framing: recovered critical IT control after a key-person continuity failure and rebuilt privileged-access, documentation and ownership practices so core systems no longer depended on one administrator.

## Durable operating model

Anton states that the technology / service operating model he built continued functioning for approximately 18 months after his departure.

This is strong management evidence because it suggests the function had repeatable processes and distributed ownership rather than depending on Anton personally.

## Highest-value CV candidates

1. Support turnaround: long / inconsistent handling -> routine issues often 10–15 minutes, with explicit reaction and resolution SLAs.
2. Demand reduction: roughly 300–600 requests/month -> ~150/month over about 6–12 months through standardization, training and root-cause reduction.
3. Event operations: fragmented email / phone / Excel -> one tracked cross-department workflow with shared calendar and equipment reservation; roughly 300 events processed.
4. Identity risk: 160+ stale former-employee accounts / mailboxes -> controlled offboarding and HR-linked access removal.
5. License / certificate continuity: surprise expiry and reporting disruption -> planned renewal / procurement cycle.
6. Product-fit judgment: Bitrix24 tested but rejected as too complex; process work shifted toward Pyrus.
7. Key-person risk: uncontrolled dependency on one administrator -> centralized privileged-access and ownership model.
8. Cross-functional digitization: finance, legal, procurement and technical workflows moved toward structured digital requests / approvals.
9. Durable management system: operating model remained functional for roughly 18 months after leadership handover.
